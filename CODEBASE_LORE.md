## I. Senomagos

Senomagos — the old market — still opens her gates every morning. It is important to say that
first, because of what follows: she works, people live there, business is done. She was built
the way cities are built — nobody planned her, everybody added.

At the gate stand four officials. One opens the door. One translates what you said into the
proper form. One notarises the form. One remembers where things are kept. Each of them
competent, each of them necessary on the day he was appointed, each of them stamping.

A traveller who came to ask the Archive one single question spent almost the whole visit at the
gate.

The Archive itself sits at the back, mostly idle, and has done for years. It was never the
problem. This is the part everyone got wrong, and got wrong for a long time, because the Archive
is old and the gate is new and it is obvious which of those you blame.

Eight hundred and seventy-seven travellers a day, and not one of them turned away by the
Archivist.

## II. The Surveyor

Eventually somebody went out with a stopwatch.

He had gone to answer a small question — whether a certain fast road was worth paving — and he
came back with the answer to a question nobody had asked. He had run the same errand three
times, on three different roads, and written the times in a table. The table said the gate cost
more than the Archive. It said so in the first row, and then said it again in every row after
that.

The table said two other things, and both were inconvenient.

The first: the fast road was genuinely fast, but only while nothing had to be unpacked and
repacked at the border. Goods that had to be handed over in a form the travellers could read
lost at the border most of what the road had saved.

The second, written at the bottom where conclusions go: none of the roads mattered until
somebody found out how many travellers there would actually be. He did not know. He wrote that
down too, which is the difference between a surveyor and a salesman.

## III. Sirodunon and Eporedon

You cannot rebuild a city while people are living in it. So: empty ground, one street plan, and
— this is the part visitors always ask about — two cities built from it.

The founder had understood one thing about himself that founders usually leave out of the
charter: one day he will not be here. Everything else follows from that sentence, including the
names. They were chosen before the first stone and are job descriptions rather than boasts: the
fort that stands, and the city of chariots.

**Sirodunon** is built so that a stranger can be handed the keys. Every street is signed, every
building says what it is for, and nothing in her requires having known the founder. She is not
the fast city. She is the *true* one: where the two disagree, Sirodunon is right, by charter,
without argument.

**Eporedon** has the same streets in half the space, packed like a ship's hold. One hand built
her and one hand understands her, and the charter is blunt about what that means: she may fall
behind, and she may be abandoned entirely on any morning without a single thing having to
change. No street may exist only in Eporedon. No errand may begin in one city and finish in the
other.

That last rule is not tidiness. A traveller who tried it would remember one city's business
while standing in the other's, and would be *confidently* wrong, which is much worse than being
lost.

## IV. The Tablets, and the Mint

Two cities built from memory will disagree. Not loudly — that would be a kindness. They will
disagree in a spelling, and it will hold for a year, and then a shipment will be refused at a
border by a clerk who is entirely correct.

So the tablets. Not the architecture of either city: the spellings. What a coin weighs, what
each refusal is called, how the shelves are numbered. Sirodunon and Eporedon read the same
stone. The stone is not the law of one city or the other, it is the thing that makes them the
same city, twice.

The Mint is the same idea with the argument removed. Coins are struck in exactly one place, and
neither city is permitted her own press, however convenient. Two mints is two currencies, and
nobody finds out for a year.

## V. The empty streets

Most of the new ground is signposts. Streets named, corners marked, no houses.

This is the plan drawn before the building, not a city that failed. Still, it makes for a
strange walk, and it produces the one courtesy both cities insist on: a traveller who asks for
an unbuilt street is *told* that it is not built. He is not politely walked to the town hall and
shown the door, as though a door were what he came for. He would have believed it. He would have
gone home and told people. And the correction would have arrived much, much later, from somebody
who could no longer be asked what he had actually wanted.

## VI. Asking Senomagos

Senomagos is not a ruin. She is the last witness.

When nobody in the new cities can remember how something is *supposed* to behave — not how it
was built, how it should behave — somebody walks over and asks. Senomagos always knows. She has
been doing that particular thing every morning for years and has no opinion about whether it is
elegant.

The answer is then carved, immediately, on the way back. Not because carving is satisfying, but
because Senomagos is patient and will not be there forever, and a question asked twice gets two
answers.

## VII. The desks

Neither city keeps its business in its head. This is the one arrangement Sirodunon and
Eporedon share down to the smallest habit, which is worth noticing, because almost nothing
else about them is alike.

A traveller arrives, and a desk is set out for him. Onto it goes what he has actually asked
for, and nothing else. Nobody fetches a man's whole life out of the Archive because he wanted
to know what day it is — the runners go when there is something to fetch, and not before.

Everything on that desk is a copy. The whole arrangement rests on that sentence, and both
cities are severe about it: **the desk may burn.** If it does, the clerk walks to the Archive
and sets it out again, and what was lost is the walk. Nothing is ever *only* on a desk. A city
where the desk is the truth is a city that burns down once.

The desks are small on purpose. A clerk keeps about two pages of a ledger — the one in front
of him and the next — and walks for older pages when somebody actually asks to look back. A
clerk who copies out five hundred rows because he might need them has not been thorough. He
has taken the floor that eight other desks were going to stand on.

And every desk is cleared ten minutes after it was set out. Not ten minutes after it was last
touched: ten minutes after it was *set out*, whatever the clerk happens to be in the middle
of. It looks wasteful, and it is. It is insurance. A clerk who missed a correction is wrong
for ten minutes and then he is empty, which is a much better kind of wrong.

Sometimes a traveller comes back and is sent to a different clerk, whose desk is bare. That
costs him a walk. It does not cost him a wrong answer, and both cities decided long ago which
of the two they were willing to pay.

## VIII. Two kinds of news

A clerk learns that something has changed in one of two ways, and the cities handle them
differently on purpose.

**He changed it himself.** Then he knows exactly what changed, because he did it, and he
corrects his own desk on the spot — the entry, and the total it belongs to. That case is cheap
because the knowing and the doing were in the same hands.

**Somebody else changed it.** Here the tempting thing is a register: a list of every desk in
the city holding a copy of that document, so that all of them can be visited. Both cities
refuse. The list is more work than the thing it protects, it is out of date the moment another
desk is set out, and keeping it correct is a full day's employment for a clerk who serves no
travellers at all.

Instead the *document* carries a mark, and the mark changes when the document does. The clerk
notices when he next reaches for his copy — not sooner, because sooner, nobody needed it. He
holds the two marks side by side, sees that his is behind, and walks.

Precise correction where somebody knew. Late discovery where nobody did. Neither one requires
a register.

## IX. The ledger that only grows

The ledger is the happy case, and it earns a chapter because it shows what the marks are for.

Nothing is ever struck out of it. Entries are added, in order, numbered.

So a clerk never asks the useless question — *is everything I copied still true?* — which for
a ledger can only be answered by copying all of it again. He asks where he stopped. He stopped
at four thousand seven hundred and eleven. The ledger stands at four thousand seven hundred
and seventeen. He copies six lines and is current.

That is the whole trick, and both cities give it a deliberately boring name: catching up. What
elsewhere would have meant throwing away everything good along with the one thing stale is,
here, six lines and a short walk.

## X. Before anything is copied down

Not everything on a desk deserves the same treatment, and the shortest road to a wrong city is
one rule for all of it.

So before a clerk copies anything, he asks. The questions hang on a board in every office, in
the same order in both cities. Who can change this? Can I? Can somebody else? Is it only ever
added to? Does it carry a mark? Can I fetch just the part I am missing? And last, and heaviest:
how fresh does this actually have to be?

The answers sort themselves out. His own things he keeps, and corrects himself. Ledgers he
catches up on. What others may change, he checks the mark. What never changes, he keeps as
long as he likes. And what goes stale while you are looking at it he does not copy at all — he
walks, every time, and the walking is the point.

The board ends with a line that sounds like a joke and is not: the wrong answer is to have one
answer.

## XI. Quarters, and who does what

Both new cities are laid out by trade rather than by material. The whole of the community
business stands in one quarter — its records, its arithmetic, its named operations together —
instead of every record in the city on one street and every calculation on another. Somebody
looking for how a member joins a community walks to the community quarter and finds it, rather
than visiting three streets and assembling it himself.

Most of those quarters are still the empty streets of chapter V, and that changes nothing: new
community business belongs in the community quarter, and the fact that it will stand there
alone is not a reason to put it somewhere livelier.

Inside a quarter there are three kinds of thing, and the cities are strict about not letting
them blur:

**The records** — what a thing is. A member. A transaction. A community.

**The small arithmetic** — sums done on a record that nobody would call an event: what a
balance comes to, what has decayed since. These need no ceremony and are given none.

**The named business** — one door, one name, and the name is the one people actually use.
*Add a member. Create a transaction. Cancel a transaction.* One piece of business, one door,
named after itself and never after the machinery behind it.

The last of these carries the city. Somebody who reads nothing but the names on those doors
should be able to say what the city does for a living. That is the test, and it is the reason
a door is never labelled with the tools in the room.

And one division of labour that is easy to get wrong: the clerk decides **when** something is
needed, the runner knows **where it lies and how to carry it**. The clerk never learns the
shelf numbers. The runner is never asked whether a document is fresh enough — to answer that
he would have to guess what it was wanted for, and he would guess wrong.

## XII. The board in the square

Some things every traveller asks for, and copying them onto four hundred desks would be an
odd way to spend a morning. Those are posted in the square: what communities there are, what
the house rules say, the public list of what has been given.

The board is not the Archive either. Nobody is allowed to forget that. It is a convenience,
and it is treated like one — whoever changes something rewrites the board of his own office
before he sits back down, and no office is ever more than ten minutes behind the others. No
runners go between offices to correct boards. That was considered, once, and priced.

Which is the oldest rule in both cities, and the one every other rule turns out to be a form
of: the fastest errand is the one nobody has to run.

## XIII. Founding a city in an afternoon

Founding a city used to require a founder who already knew about cities.

To raise another Senomagos somewhere you need eleven separate works, put up in the right
order and taught to find one another: the halls, the archive, a strongroom for what is only
remembered briefly, the messengers who talk to other cities, the man at the gate with his four
sheets of instructions, and two roads that must be kept from ever seeing each other. There is
a book about how to do it. There are two, in fact — one for your own ground and one for ground
you rent.

None of that is wrong. All of it is why almost nobody founds one.

So the charter of the new cities set a requirement stranger than any of the others, and it is
the one that people who care nothing for architecture ask about first: **a city must fit in a
single file, and someone who does not understand cities must be able to found one.**

You carry the file to a piece of ground. You start it. It digs its own well, asks you the one
question it cannot answer for itself — who are you, this community? — and then there is a
city, and it is yours.

The one question turned out to be eleven, which is what happens to one questions. Where the
well is, who carries the letters, the word that opens the carrier's bag. And most of the
eleven were being answered identically, in the same order, by people raising a city on a
bench for the afternoon and pulling it down again before supper.

So the file learned to ask the *first* question first: is this a city, or is this a city on a
bench? For a bench it then writes out all eleven itself and holds the sheet up — this name,
this ground, the well it dug, letters to the box in the corner rather than to anybody alive —
walks over to the box to check that somebody is sitting in it, comes back and says so, and
asks one thing. Yes or no. A founder who says no is asked the eleven one at a time, with the
sensible answer already written in the margin of each; pressing on takes it.

Everything the founder said that is *not* who the city is — which well, which letter-carrier,
the word for the bag — is written on a note nailed inside the gate. The city reads that note
each morning before it does anything else, and it never argues with an instruction the
magistrate has already shouted over the wall: what was shouted stands, and the note fills in
the rest. Both cities read the same note in the same hand, which is what lets a founder pull
one down and raise the other on the same ground without writing anything twice.

The name is not on the note and never will be. A name on a note is a name two people can
disagree about, and there is exactly one place a city's name is allowed to live.

The pictures live in a chest beside it. Not in the book: Senomagos kept the faces of her
people *inside* the archive, so every copy anyone made of that archive carried everybody's
face along with it, and reading one line meant lifting all of them. The new cities write a key
in the book and put the picture in the chest.

A founder who *does* know about cities gives the same file a deep cistern instead of the well,
and a granary instead of the chest. It is still the same file, and what stands is not a
different city. That is the whole of the arrangement, and it is worth saying plainly because
it is easy to build the other way round: the knowledge is optional, and the ceiling is not
lowered for the people who do not have it.

## XIV. One book, and the scribe who is not needed

Senomagos writes a transfer down three times.

Once in her own book, in her own hand, because that is the book her clerks read. Once more on
the shelf of things not yet certain, in the same hand and nearly the same words, waiting to
become the first one. And a third time — this is the interesting one — in the script of the
great register that stands outside all the cities and belongs to none of them, carried out
there by a scribe who is the only person alive who reads both hands.

Then there is a fourth book, a thin one, in which somebody notes whether the first and the
third agree.

They usually do. When they do not, the argument is never about which entry is wrong. It is
about which of the four books you would trust to tell you.

The new cities looked at that arrangement for a long time and then made the change that sounds
smaller than it is: **they write in the register's hand from the start.** One shape, signed
once. The city's book is not a translation of what stands in the register — it is the same
entry, kept where it is convenient to read. Making a transfer and confirming one stop being
two crafts, because they were only ever two crafts on account of the two scripts.

There is no scribe. Not because he was bad at the work — he was the best of them — but because
a man who is the only one able to compare two things is also the only one able to be wrong
about them, and that is a great deal to ask of one man for a hundred years.

## XV. Asking the letter-carrier whether he is awake

Every morning, before the gates are open to anybody, a runner goes down to the letter-carrier's
house.

He knocks. He says which city he is from. If the bag needs a word he gives the word. And then
— this is the part worth having — he hands over no letter at all, wishes the man a good day,
and walks back.

He goes by the road the letters go by, and not by the footpath over the hill, and that is not
thoroughness. A carrier will open his door to a man who came over the hill and turn away the
cart that arrives on the road, for reasons entirely about the road; a runner who took the
short way would come back saying all is well every morning of a month in which nothing was
delivered. One line goes on the board: *he answered*, or *he did not, and these were his
words*.

The city opens either way. A city that cannot send letters can still do every single thing
that is not sending a letter, and there is no version of this where the gates stay shut over
it. What there is a version of, and what the runner exists to prevent, is finding out three
weeks later from a citizen who never got hers — and then spending an afternoon establishing
which of the eleven answers was the wrong one.

A city that was never given a letter-carrier says that too, in as many words, on the same
board. Silence there would be indistinguishable from a carrier nobody thought to check.
